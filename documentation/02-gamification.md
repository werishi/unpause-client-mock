# Gamification

> Covers README section: 2. Gentle gamification

## Summary

Adding gamification to depression apps does not clearly improve symptoms or adherence, and in other health areas its small to medium effects fade once the game ends. Recording progress reliably helps people reach goals, and positive feedback supports motivation, while expected rewards can weaken interest in an activity. Depression blunts reward sensitivity and willingness to spend effort, and biases memory towards the negative, so progress is easy to overlook. For Unpause, this supports points as gentle feedback on small, achievable steps, self-chosen rewards that are themselves meaningful activities, comebacks framed as a fresh start and concrete progress reviews, and it argues against loss framing, broken streaks and competition, with gamification tested rather than assumed to work.

## Key findings

### Gamification in mental health and health apps

- A meta-analysis of 38 studies (8,110 participants, 50 comparisons) found that mental health apps reduced depressive symptoms compared with controls (Hedges g = −0.27, a small effect). The number of gamification elements in an app did not predict symptom reduction (β = −0.03, P = .38) or adherence (P = .40). Longer intervention duration did predict adherence. Heterogeneity was high, some studies were at high risk of bias, there were signs of publication bias, and participants were not limited to people with diagnosed depression ([Six et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/32199)).
- A systematic review of 70 papers covering 50 mental health and well-being apps found that the most common elements were levels or progress feedback (80%), points (56%), rewards (50%) and narrative (48%). Only 59% of papers explained why gamification was used, mostly "to encourage use". The authors judged the evidence for points, progress feedback and rewards as unclear, and personalization and customization as more promising. They described gamification in this field as rarely theory-driven ([Cheng et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.2196/13717)).
- In an RCT with 283 adults with significant depressive symptoms, a gamified self-help app (SuperBetter, 10 minutes a day for one month) reduced symptoms more than a waitlist (d = 0.67 after the program, d = 1.05 at follow-up). Both active arms were gamified, so the trial cannot show whether gamification was the active ingredient. The authors urge caution because of high attrition and a self-selected, motivated sample ([Roepke et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1089/g4h.2014.0046)).
- A meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (2,407 participants) found that gamified interventions increased physical activity (g = 0.42). Compared with a non-gamified activity program, the gain was smaller (g = 0.23). About 14 weeks after the game ended, the effect was small (g = 0.15). Effects did not differ between healthy people and adults with chronic disease. Depression was not studied ([Mazeas et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.2196/26779)).
- In the STEP UP RCT (602 adults with overweight or obesity, 24 weeks), points and levels with social incentives increased daily steps compared with tracking alone (competition +920, support +689, collaboration +637 steps per day). The points used loss framing: participants got 70 points every Monday and lost 10 for each day they missed their goal. Those who fell to the lowest levels were reset to the middle level at weeks 8 and 16. After the game stopped, activity dropped in all arms and only the competition arm stayed significantly above control. The study did not compare loss framing with gain framing and did not include people selected for depression ([Patel et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.3505)).

### Points, rewards and motivation

- A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that expected tangible rewards for doing, completing or performing well on a task reduced later free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = −0.40, −0.36 and −0.28). Positive feedback, in contrast, increased both free-choice behavior and self-reported interest. Tangible rewards were more harmful for children than for college students. The experiments did not focus on people with depression ([Deci et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)).
- A meta-analysis of 23 RCTs (6,074 adults) found that modest financial incentives (about US$1.40 a day) increased daily steps while they were in place (+607 steps). After incentives stopped, the pooled effect was still positive (+514 steps), but only 4 of 18 studies reported a benefit after the incentive ended. The two results do not fully agree, so long-term effects remain uncertain ([Mitchell et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-100633)).
- A systematic review on self-incentives and self-rewards found only a very small effect for self-incentives (d = 0.17, 7 studies, 1,161 participants). No study on self-rewards met the inclusion criteria, so there is no pooled evidence on whether rewarding yourself changes behavior ([Brown et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2017.09.004)).

### Reward processing and effort in depression

- A model-based meta-analysis of six datasets (392 sessions) found that major depression and anhedonia mainly reduce reward sensitivity, meaning how strongly a reward is felt, rather than the ability to learn from rewards. The datasets were small, so the result is provisional ([Huys et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/2045-5380-3-12)).
- A meta-analysis of 68 studies (3,697 participants) found that people with major depression are less willing to spend effort to obtain rewards than healthy controls (g = −0.28, a small effect). The deficit was larger in schizophrenia spectrum and bipolar disorders ([Pillny et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000510)).

### Progress feedback and reviews

- A meta-analysis of 138 randomized studies (19,951 participants) found that interventions that prompt people to monitor their goal progress increased monitoring (d = 1.98) and improved goal attainment (d = 0.40). Effects were larger when progress was physically recorded and when it was reported to others or made public. Most goals were health goals such as weight loss or smoking. Depression was not a focus ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)).
- A meta-analysis of 153 studies found that people with depression show a small explicit memory bias for negative over positive material (g = 0.24). In remitted depression, the bias was not significant under normal conditions but appeared after mood or stress induction. Heterogeneity was high. The studies used lab material such as word lists, not memory of one's own achievements ([Everaert et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000367)).

### Streaks, missed days and fresh starts

- Seven studies found that showing people an intact streak in a behavior log increased later engagement more than showing a broken streak, regardless of how much they had actually done. The drop after a broken streak was larger when people blamed themselves for the break, and smaller when they could "repair" the streak. Participants were general consumers, not people with depression ([Silverman & Barasch, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029)).
- Three archival field studies found that searches for "diet", gym visits and goal commitments increase after temporal landmarks such as the start of a week, month, year or semester, a birthday or a holiday. The authors propose that landmarks open a new mental period and put past failures behind people. The data are observational and from general populations ([Dai et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901)).
- In a 12-week study of 96 volunteers forming a new daily habit, missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. Time to reach a stable habit varied widely (18 to 254 days). The model fit well for only 39 participants, so this is a small, non-clinical sample ([Lally et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)).

## Implications for Unpause

### Overall role of gamification

- **Treat gamification as a support layer, not the treatment.** The evidence-based core is behavioral activation. Gamification did not add to the effect of depression apps or to adherence ([Six et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/32199)), and in health apps it is often added without a clear rationale ([Cheng et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.2196/13717)). Every game element should have a stated purpose tied to activation, such as making progress visible.
- **Do not judge success by app usage.** Unpause wants less screen time. Measure whether users complete real-world tasks and whether mood improves, not how often they open the app. Plan to test gamification against a plain version, for example with an A/B test, rather than assume it helps.
- **Expect effects to fade.** Gamification and incentive effects shrink when the game or the reward stops ([Patel et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.3505); [Mazeas et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.2196/26779); [Mitchell et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2019-100633)). Points should gradually hand over to things that last: habits, values and the user's own experience that activity lifts mood.

### Points

- **Frame points as feedback, not as payment.** Positive, informational feedback raised intrinsic interest, while expected tangible rewards lowered it ([Deci et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)). Copy should say what the user did and why it matters ("You went outside today. That counts."), not "earn 10 points to unlock".
- **Make points easy to get on a bad day.** Depression reduces both reward sensitivity and willingness to spend effort ([Huys et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/2045-5380-3-12); [Pillny et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000510)). Award points for the two-minute version of a task, and for starting, not only for finishing. Keep the first reward goal close.
- **Do not count on points feeling rewarding.** With blunted reward sensitivity, a point total may feel flat. Pair points with the before-and-after mood rating, so the user sees the effect of the activity itself.
- **Gain only, never loss.** STEP UP used loss-framed points with success, but in non-depressed adults and without a gain-framed comparison ([Patel et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.3505)). Given the evidence on broken streaks and self-blame ([Silverman & Barasch, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029)), and the README principle that missed days are never punished, Unpause should never deduct points or show a balance going down.
- **No leaderboards by default.** Competition worked best in STEP UP, but there is no evidence that public ranking is safe for people with depression. If the buddy feature uses points, prefer support-style sharing (a trusted person sees progress) over ranking.

### Comeback bonus instead of streaks

- **Do not show broken streaks.** Showing a broken streak lowers later engagement, more so when people blame themselves ([Silverman & Barasch, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029)). If Unpause shows any continuity, count active days in a period ("12 active days this month"), which a missed day cannot "break".
- **Frame the comeback as a fresh start.** Allowing a streak to be repaired softened the drop ([Silverman & Barasch, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac029)), and new periods help people leave past failures behind ([Dai et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901)). The comeback bonus does both. Combine it with a clean start of the week or month and a gentle "welcome back", with no reference to how long the user was away.
- **Be honest that one missed day does not matter much.** Missing one opportunity did not materially slow habit formation ([Lally et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)). The app can say this, while avoiding claims about longer breaks, which were not tested.
- **Use temporal landmarks.** Offer reviews, new plans and fresh-start prompts on Mondays and at the start of a month ([Dai et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2014.1901)). STEP UP's mid-game reset of low-level players is a design precedent ([Patel et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.3505)).
- **Keep the bonus modest.** As a design judgment, the comeback bonus should welcome people back without making a break more rewarding than steady small steps.

### Real-world rewards

- **Let users choose, but present rewards as a nice extra.** There is no pooled evidence that self-rewards work, and self-incentives have a very small effect ([Brown et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2017.09.004)). Rewards should stay optional.
- **Suggest rewards that are themselves activating.** The README example, a cinema night with a friend, is a social, values-linked activity. Rewards like this double as behavioral activation. Avoid suggesting rewards that work against the user's goals, such as extra hours of streaming.
- **Keep reward thresholds near.** Low effort willingness in depression ([Pillny et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000510)) argues for several small, near goals rather than one distant one.
- **Do not attach heavy rewards to activities the user already enjoys.** Expected tangible rewards can reduce intrinsic interest ([Deci et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)). For values-linked activities the user likes, lead with meaning and mood feedback rather than points.

### "Look how far you've come" reviews

- **Show concrete records, not just a score.** Recording progress improves goal attainment ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)), and memory in depression leans towards the negative ([Everaert et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000367)). Reviews should list what the user actually did, how mood changed around tasks, and which values they served.
- **Compare to the starting point, not last week.** A bad week compared with a good one may feel like failure. Cumulative views ("since you started") are kinder and still accurate.
- **Optional sharing.** Progress had larger effects when reported to others ([Harkin et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025)). Sharing reviews with a therapist or buddy can help, but only by explicit opt-in, in line with the privacy principle.

## Evidence gaps and risks

- **No direct evidence for the specific features.** No trial has tested points, a comeback bonus, self-set real-world rewards or progress reviews in people with depression. The design is built on evidence from other populations and on theory.
- **Gamification evidence in depression is weak.** The main meta-analysis found no added benefit from gamification and had high heterogeneity, risk of bias and possible publication bias ([Six et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/32199)). Single trials of gamified apps used waitlist controls, had high attrition and could not separate gamification from content ([Roepke et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1089/g4h.2014.0046)).
- **Most evidence comes from non-depressed people.** Physical activity trials, consumer studies, lab experiments with students and habit studies with healthy volunteers may not transfer to people with anhedonia and low energy.
- **Loss versus gain framing is not settled.** Loss-framed points worked in STEP UP. Unpause's gain-only approach is a reasoned choice based on related findings and its design principles, not a tested result. The abstinence-violation and "what-the-hell" effects from addiction and dieting research are often cited for this point, but they were not reviewed here.
- **Undermining intrinsic motivation.** Rewards could reduce interest in activities the user would otherwise enjoy ([Deci et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)). It is unclear how these lab findings apply to people with anhedonia, for whom many activities may hold little interest at first.
- **Points may feel empty or like failure.** With blunted reward sensitivity, points may not motivate. Missing a reward goal could confirm negative beliefs about oneself. Thresholds that are too high are a specific risk.
- **Progress reviews can backfire.** In a depressive episode, a review that shows little activity could lower mood. Reviews need a gentle path for weeks with little progress, and a link to the safety plan if mood ratings fall sharply.
- **Engagement can become the goal.** Game elements can drive app use or compulsive checking instead of real-world activity. That would conflict with the less-screen-time principle and should be monitored.
- **Comeback bonus side effects are unknown.** Whether a bonus could reward breaks, or feel patronizing, has not been studied.
- **Long-term effects are unclear.** Effects of gamification and incentives fade after they stop, and long-term follow-up is short in most studies.
