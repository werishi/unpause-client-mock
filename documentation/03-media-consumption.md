# Media Consumption

> Covers README section: 3. Get what you care about without the endless scroll

## Summary

Short randomized trials show that cutting back on social media or smartphone time leads to a small drop in depressive symptoms, and limiting use works at least as well as quitting. Evidence on news, TV and binge-watching is mostly correlational, but heavy distressing news and mentally passive viewing are consistently tied to worse mood and more stress. Batching notifications into a few fixed times improved mood and control in a field trial, while switching them off completely raised anxiety. For Unpause, this supports intent-based content, fixed digest times and a soft, adjustable budget, with modest expectations: using less media is a supporting step, not a treatment for depression on its own.

## Key findings

### Reducing social media and screen time

- A meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (N = 1,491) found that reducing or stopping social media use lowered depressive symptoms. The effect was small (Hedges' g = 0.25 after correcting for publication bias). Limiting use had a significant effect (g = 0.33), full abstinence did not (g = 0.15), but the difference between the two was not significant. Interventions lasted only 7 to 21 days. 9 of 10 trials did not require participants to have depressive symptoms, and the one that did showed the largest effect ([May et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15110222)).
- In an RCT with 143 undergraduates, limiting Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to 10 minutes per platform per day for three weeks reduced loneliness and depression compared with usual use. Both groups had less anxiety and fear of missing out, which may be an effect of simply tracking one's own use ([Hunt et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751)).
- In an RCT with 111 healthy students, limiting smartphone screen time to 2 hours a day or less for three weeks improved depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), stress, sleep and well-being, with small to medium effects. Screen time went back up quickly after the trial ended. The trial was not blinded and used self-reported outcomes ([Pieh et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-03944-z)).
- In a large randomized experiment with US Facebook users, deactivating Facebook for four weeks increased subjective well-being. People spent more time offline, including socializing with family and friends but also watching TV alone. Facebook use stayed lower after the experiment ended. Deactivation also reduced factual news knowledge ([Allcott et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658)).
- A systematic review of "digital detox" (21 studies, 3,625 participants, 12 RCTs) found mixed results. Some studies showed benefits, some showed no effect, and some showed negative effects on well-being ([Radtke et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/20501579211028647)).

### What kind of use matters

- A systematic review of 23 studies found that problematic smartphone use was consistently related to depression severity, with at least medium effect sizes. Links with anxiety were smaller. All included studies were correlational ([Elhai et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2016.08.030)).
- In three large datasets of adolescents (n = 355,358), digital technology use explained at most 0.4% of the variation in well-being. Whether the link looked negative, positive or absent depended heavily on analysis choices ([Orben & Przybylski, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1)).
- A review of 40 studies found that most did not support the popular idea that "active" social media use helps and "passive" use harms. The authors argue that time-based measures are too coarse. Content, the user's mood and individual sensitivity likely matter more ([Valkenburg et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448211065425)).

### News and doomscrolling

- In a representative survey of 4,675 US adults after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombings, six or more hours a day of bombing-related media exposure in the first week was linked to higher acute stress than being directly present at the event. This is a cross-sectional study ([Holman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1316265110)).
- A scoping review of 17 studies found that doomscrolling is consistently linked to anxiety, depression, stress and lower resilience. Doomscrolling is described as habitual and reinforced by endless feeds and algorithmic content. Almost all studies were cross-sectional. The only experimental intervention found short-term benefits only ([Sharpe et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1108/MHDT-10-2025-0068)).

### TV, video and binge-watching

- A meta-analysis of 12 prospective studies (128,553 participants) linked TV viewing to a higher risk of depression (RR 1.18). Computer use was not linked. The authors suggest "mentally passive" sitting is the problem. Studies that adjusted for baseline depression showed no significant link, so reverse causation is possible ([Huang et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-020-0715-z)).
- A meta-analysis of 16 cross-sectional studies (8,077 adults) found a weak link between binge-watching and depression (pooled estimate 0.14) and somewhat stronger links with anxiety and stress. Most included studies were rated low quality, and heterogeneity was high ([Alimoradi et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19159707)).

### Notifications and interruptions

- In a randomized field experiment (N = 237), people whose phone notifications were batched three times a day felt more attentive, in a better mood and more in control of their phone. They also reported lower stress and fewer interruptions than the control group. People who got no notifications at all gained few of these benefits and reported more anxiety and fear of missing out ([Fitz et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016)).

### Infinite scroll and feed design

- A one-week field study (N = 46) described infinite scrolling as a "loop" that stretches social media sessions and leaves users with regret. Most reasons people stopped scrolling came from their surroundings, not from the app. The authors conclude that interventions should take the user's context into account ([Rixen et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3604275)).

### Habits, plans and rewards

- In a preregistered RCT with 787 students, forming action plans and coping plans did not reduce objectively measured total smartphone time. Higher self-efficacy after the intervention was linked to lower later use ([Brockmeier et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108624)).
- In a field experiment on "temptation bundling", people who could listen to gripping audiobooks only at the gym went to the gym 51% more often than controls. A group that was only encouraged to do this went 29% more often. The effects faded over time. Afterwards, 61% chose to pay for gym-only access to the audiobooks ([Milkman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784)).

## Implications for Unpause

1. **Aim to reduce, not to quit.** Limiting use worked at least as well as full abstinence ([May et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15110222)), and effective limits in trials were moderate (about 30 minutes of social media a day in [Hunt et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751); 2 hours of total smartphone time in [Pieh et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-03944-z)). The media budget should start from the user's own current use and suggest a moderate cut. It should never default to zero.

2. **Build for the long run and expect rebound.** No trial lasted longer than three weeks, and screen time rebounded quickly when a trial ended ([Pieh et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-03944-z)). The budget should be an ongoing, adjustable setting, not a one-week challenge. Show trends over weeks rather than daily pass/fail.

3. **Batch the digest, but do not go fully silent.** Fixed digest times match the batching condition that improved mood and control ([Fitz et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016)). In the same study, removing notifications completely raised anxiety and fear of missing out. So: let users pick one to three digest times, send a single "your digest is ready" message at that time, and send nothing in between.

4. **Give the digest a clear end.** Infinite scroll keeps people in a loop they later regret ([Rixen et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3604275)), and doomscrolling is tied to endless feeds ([Sharpe et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1108/MHDT-10-2025-0068)). Each digest should be a short, finite list with a clear "You're all caught up" end. No infinite feed, no "more like this" rabbit holes, and no autoplay.

5. **Let intent drive content, not minutes alone.** What people consume and how they feel seem to matter more than raw screen time ([Valkenburg et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448211065425); [Orben & Przybylski, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1)), and problematic, compulsive use is more strongly linked to depression than time spent ([Elhai et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2016.08.030)). Interest-based search ("News about financing of WoW Forever") puts the user's own intent first. This is the core of the feature and should stay central.

6. **Handle distressing news with care.** Heavy, repeated exposure to distressing news is linked to acute stress ([Holman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1316265110)) and doomscrolling to anxiety and depression ([Sharpe et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1108/MHDT-10-2025-0068)). Prefer short text summaries over graphic images and video, cap the number of items per topic, and offer an optional "go easy on heavy news" filter. At the same time, keep users informed. Cutting social media reduced news knowledge in [Allcott et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658), and a curated digest can avoid that trade-off.

7. **Treat video as discrete episodes.** TV viewing and binge-watching show small, mostly correlational links with depression ([Huang et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-020-0715-z); [Alimoradi et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19159707)). Count video in episodes, not open-ended minutes, and never auto-start the next one. Ending each episode with a stopping point makes the "Earn your episode" idea concrete.

8. **Design "Earn your episode" as pairing first, earning second.** The tested approach is temptation bundling: enjoy something only while doing a useful activity ([Milkman et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784)). Unpause can suggest pairings like "your podcast, but only on your walk". Earning extra time for completed tasks is plausible but not directly tested. Keep bonus time small, never take away the base budget as a penalty, and expect the novelty to fade.

9. **Make swap suggestions concrete and context-aware.** Freed-up time does not automatically go to healthy activities. In [Allcott et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658) some of it went to watching TV alone. Writing your own plans did not reduce phone use ([Brockmeier et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108624)), so Unpause should offer ready-made, low-effort swaps instead of asking users to plan them. Because people stop scrolling mostly for reasons in their surroundings ([Rixen et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3604275)), swaps should fit the time of day, place and energy level from the morning check-in. Celebrating a successful swap may build the self-efficacy that predicted lower use in [Brockmeier et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108624).

10. **Do not moralize screen time.** Effects are small on average ([Orben & Przybylski, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1); [May et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15110222)) and some detox studies found negative effects ([Radtke et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/20501579211028647)). Use neutral, kind language and avoid "addiction" labels. As a product choice that fits the "Stay connected" section, consider not counting direct messages with friends against the budget, since the budget is meant to curb feeds and video, not contact. The evidence on "active" versus "passive" use is mixed ([Valkenburg et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448211065425)), so this is a design judgment, not a proven rule.

11. **Measure whether it helps this group.** The benefit may be larger in people with more depressive symptoms ([May et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15110222)), but this is barely studied. Link media budget data with the PHQ-9 trend from the therapist check-ins so users and therapists can see whether the changes help.

## Evidence gaps and risks

- **Short trials.** No RCT in the main meta-analysis lasted longer than three weeks, and only three measured outcomes after the intervention ended ([May et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15110222)). Use rebounds quickly ([Pieh et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-03944-z)). Long-term effects are unknown.
- **Wrong population.** Almost all trials used students or community volunteers, often motivated and with low symptom levels. Only one trial required depressive symptoms, and none studied problematic users ([May et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15110222)). Results may not transfer to people with clinical depression.
- **Mostly correlational outside social media.** Evidence on TV, binge-watching, doomscrolling and problematic smartphone use is mostly cross-sectional. Reverse causation is likely: depressed people may watch more because they are depressed ([Huang et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-020-0715-z); [Alimoradi et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19159707); [Sharpe et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1108/MHDT-10-2025-0068)).
- **Small effects, weak measures.** Pooled effects are small, outcomes are self-reported, and trials are not blinded. Associations between screen time and well-being are tiny and sensitive to analysis choices ([Orben & Przybylski, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1)).
- **Design features barely tested.** We found no peer-reviewed journal study showing that removing autoplay or infinite scroll improves mood. The feed-design evidence here is one small field study ([Rixen et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3604275)).
- **Unpause's specific features are untested.** No study has tested an interest-based, curated digest, a media budget with earned bonus time, or five-minute swap prompts for depression. These are reasonable extrapolations, not proven interventions. Planning-based approaches to cut phone use failed in one large trial ([Brockmeier et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108624)).
- **Possible harms.**
  - Cutting media can backfire for some people ([Radtke et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/20501579211028647)). Removing all notifications raised anxiety and fear of missing out ([Fitz et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016)).
  - Less social media can mean less news knowledge ([Allcott et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658)) and, for isolated users, less contact with others.
  - Going over the budget may trigger self-criticism, which is common in depression. Overruns must never be framed as failure.
  - "Earn your episode" could make media feel even more valuable, or make users feel they do not "deserve" rest. Base time should never depend on productivity.
  - Unpause is itself a screen app. Tracking, digests and swaps add screen time, so in-app sessions should stay short.

## Sources

1. May, W. et al. (2025). *Reducing Social Media Use Decreases Depression Symptoms: A Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials*. European Journal of Investigation in Health, Psychology and Education. [Link](https://doi.org/10.3390/ejihpe15110222) — meta-analysis of 10 RCTs (N = 1,491). The main causal evidence that limiting social media modestly reduces depressive symptoms, and that limiting works at least as well as quitting.
2. Hunt, M. G. et al. (2018). *No More FOMO: Limiting Social Media Decreases Loneliness and Depression*. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751) — RCT, 143 undergraduates, 3 weeks. Shows a concrete limit (10 minutes per platform per day) that reduced depression and loneliness.
3. Pieh, C. et al. (2025). *Smartphone screen time reduction improves mental health: a randomized controlled trial*. BMC Medicine. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-025-03944-z) — RCT, 111 healthy students, 3 weeks. Supports a daily screen time budget and shows how fast use rebounds afterwards.
4. Allcott, H. et al. (2020). *The Welfare Effects of Social Media*. American Economic Review. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658) — large randomized Facebook deactivation experiment, 4 weeks. Shows well-being gains, where freed time goes, and the trade-off with news knowledge.
5. Radtke, T. et al. (2022). *Digital detox: An effective solution in the smartphone era? A systematic literature review*. Mobile Media & Communication. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1177/20501579211028647) — systematic review of 21 studies (12 RCTs). Shows that detox effects are mixed and sometimes negative.
6. Elhai, J. D. et al. (2017). *Problematic smartphone use: A conceptual overview and systematic review of relations with anxiety and depression psychopathology*. Journal of Affective Disorders. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2016.08.030) — systematic review of 23 correlational studies. Shows that compulsive use, not just time, is linked to depression.
7. Orben, A. & Przybylski, A. K. (2019). *The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use*. Nature Human Behaviour. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1) — specification curve analysis of three large datasets (n = 355,358 adolescents). A reminder that average screen time effects are tiny, so Unpause should not moralize.
8. Valkenburg, P. M. et al. (2022). *The associations of active and passive social media use with well-being: A critical scoping review*. New Media & Society. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1177/14614448211065425) — scoping review of 40 survey studies. Shows the active vs passive split is weakly supported and content matters.
9. Holman, E. A. et al. (2014). *Media's role in broadcasting acute stress following the Boston Marathon bombings*. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1316265110) — cross-sectional representative survey (N = 4,675). Links heavy distressing news exposure to acute stress.
10. Sharpe, A. T. R. et al. (2026). *The influence of doomscrolling on mental health: a scoping review*. Mental Health and Digital Technologies. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1108/MHDT-10-2025-0068) — scoping review of 17 studies. Links doomscrolling to anxiety and depression and ties it to endless feeds.
11. Huang, Y. et al. (2020). *Sedentary behaviors and risk of depression: a meta-analysis of prospective studies*. Translational Psychiatry. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-020-0715-z) — meta-analysis of 12 prospective cohort studies (128,553 participants). Links TV viewing, but not computer use, to depression risk.
12. Alimoradi, Z. et al. (2022). *Binge-Watching and Mental Health Problems: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health. [Link](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph19159707) — meta-analysis of 16 cross-sectional studies (8,077 adults). Shows a weak link between binge-watching and depression.
13. Fitz, N. et al. (2019). *Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being*. Computers in Human Behavior. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016) — randomized field experiment (N = 237). Direct support for fixed digest times, and a warning against removing notifications completely.
14. Rixen, J. O. et al. (2023). *The Loop and Reasons to Break It: Investigating Infinite Scrolling Behaviour in Social Media Applications and Reasons to Stop*. Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1145/3604275) — field study (N = 46), 1 week. Explains how infinite scroll traps users and why swaps should be context-aware.
15. Brockmeier, L. C. et al. (2025). *Planning a digital detox: Findings from a randomized controlled trial to reduce smartphone usage time*. Computers in Human Behavior. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2025.108624) — preregistered RCT (N = 787). Shows that self-written plans alone did not reduce phone use, which argues for ready-made swaps.
16. Milkman, K. L. et al. (2014). *Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling*. Management Science. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784) — field experiment. The best available evidence for pairing enjoyable media with healthy activities ("Earn your episode").
