# Safety Plan

> Covers README section: 6. Safety plan

## Summary

Safety planning is one of the better-supported brief interventions in suicide prevention. A meta-analysis found about 43% less suicidal behaviour with safety planning, but no clear effect on suicidal thoughts. The evidence quality is low, and most studies were not done in people with depression specifically. German and UK guidelines both recommend a safety plan made together with a clinician. App-based safety plans are well accepted, but no app version has been proven to work. Reviews of existing apps found wrong crisis numbers, incomplete content and sometimes harmful content. For Unpause this means: follow the established Stanley-Brown structure closely, build it with the therapist, keep it reachable offline and outside the gamification, check crisis contacts on a schedule, and present it as a support next to professional care, never instead of it.

## Key findings

### What safety planning is and how well it works

- The Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention is a written, personal plan with six steps: (1) personal warning signs, (2) internal coping strategies, (3) people and social settings that offer distraction, (4) people who can help in a crisis, (5) professionals and urgent services to contact, and (6) lethal means counselling to make the environment safer ([Stanley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.1776)).
- In nine US veterans' emergency departments (1,640 patients), safety planning plus follow-up phone calls was linked to less suicidal behaviour over six months than usual care: 3.0% vs 5.3% (OR 0.56, number needed to treat about 44). Patients were also about twice as likely to attend outpatient mental health care. The study was not randomised, about 88% of patients were men, and event rates were low ([Stanley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.1776)).
- A meta-analysis of six studies (3,536 adults) found a relative risk of 0.57 for suicidal behaviour with safety planning-type interventions (number needed to treat 16). Signs of publication bias were found. After correction, the effect was smaller but still significant (RR 0.71). There was no significant effect on suicidal ideation (three studies, 283 people). Four of the six studies had high risk of bias. The authors note that the active ingredients, including the role of follow-up calls, are unclear ([Nuij et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.2021.50)).
- A meta-analysis of 14 studies (4,270 patients) of brief interventions in acute care found fewer later suicide attempts (OR 0.69) and better linkage to follow-up care (OR about 2.7 to 3.0). Safety planning was the most common intervention. Depression symptoms did not improve significantly ([Doupnik et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.1586)).

### Follow-up contact after a crisis

- Brief contact interventions (letters, crisis cards, phone calls, postcards) did not significantly reduce the share of people who self-harmed again or attempted suicide (OR 0.87) or suicide deaths (OR 0.58, not significant) in a meta-analysis of 12 trials. They did reduce the number of repeat episodes per person (IRR 0.66), but only three studies measured this. The authors judged the evidence not strong enough for widespread use ([Milner et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.114.147819)).
- The German depression guideline says the first days and weeks after discharge carry a high suicide risk. It strongly recommends a medical or psychotherapeutic follow-up appointment within one week of discharge after inpatient care for suicidality, and immediate contact if the person misses it. This is consensus-based, not trial-based ([NVL Unipolare Depression, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)).

### Restricting access to means

- A 10-year systematic review found that the evidence for restricting access to means has grown stronger. Controlling access to analgesics was linked to an overall decrease of about 43%, and restricting access to jumping hotspots to about 86% fewer jumping suicides. No single strategy clearly stood above the others. Evidence for internet and helpline support was judged insufficient ([Zalsman et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(16)30030-X)).
- Limiting access to the means of suicide is one of the four core interventions of the WHO LIVE LIFE approach. Another is early identification, assessment, management and follow-up of anyone affected by suicidal behaviour ([WHO, 2021](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240026629)).
- Both NICE and the German guideline include means safety as part of the safety plan. The German guideline gives stockpiled medication and access to firearms as examples ([NICE, 2022](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225); [NVL Unipolare Depression, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)).

### Digital and app-based safety plans

- A systematic review of 14 studies covering seven safety planning apps (including MYPLAN, BeyondNow and SafePlan) found that most apps followed the Stanley-Brown components and added features such as editing, sharing and distraction content. Usability ratings were mostly good. Only four small, uncontrolled pilot studies (about 3 to 22 participants) measured suicide-related outcomes, and there were no published RCTs. Engagement was low outside crises. Two apps had no lethal means guidance, and many let users build a plan without a clinician, some without any disclaimer ([Gryglewicz et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/52763)).
- In a pilot RCT with 66 hospitalised suicidal adolescents, a brief safety-planning intervention plus a companion app (BRITE) did not significantly reduce suicide attempts (16% vs 31%, p = 0.17) or ideation. The app asked for daily distress ratings and showed the safety plan and crisis contacts at the highest distress level. About 71% used the app at least once, and how often it was used was not related to attempt risk. The trial was too small to detect effects ([Kennard et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2018.17101151)).
- A meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (4,398 participants) of self-guided digital tools found a small reduction in suicidal ideation for tools that directly target suicidality (g = -0.23). Tools aimed at depression in general did not significantly reduce suicidal ideation (g = -0.12, not significant) ([Torok et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2589-7500(19)30199-2)).
- The German guideline makes no recommendation on internet- or app-based interventions for suicidality. It notes that such tools are not yet available in Germany, that almost all app-based programmes list suicidality as an exclusion criterion, and that the available depression programmes have no specific effect on suicidality. Passive monitoring by phone sensors or wearables has so far only been studied for correlations, not for effects on outcomes ([NVL Unipolare Depression, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)).

### How existing apps handle risk

- A review of suicide prevention apps found 49 with interactive features. None offered comprehensive evidence-based support, and none kept crisis contacts visible at all times. Two apps listed lethal methods, presented as things to remove access to. About 39% had bugs, about 39% had a privacy policy, and only 16% had PIN or account protection ([Larsen et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0152285)).
- A review of 69 depression and suicide prevention apps found that 6 (9%) gave wrong or non-working crisis helpline numbers. Two of these had each been downloaded more than one million times. Only 26 apps (38%) helped users make a safety plan, only 5 (7%) offered all six guideline-based strategies, and only 14% cited their sources. No chatbot recognised "I am very sad and hopeless" as a warning sign ([Martinengo et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-019-1461-z)).

### Does asking about suicidal thoughts increase risk?

- A meta-analysis of 18 studies found that being asked about suicide or exposed to suicide-related content in research was linked to a small reduction in suicidal ideation (g = -0.13) and lower odds of suicidal behaviour (OR 0.71). The reduction was larger in adolescents than in adults ([Blades et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2018.07.001)).
- A second meta-analysis of 13 prospective studies found no significant harmful effect of assessing suicidality ([DeCou & Schumann, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/sltb.12368)).
- The German guideline lists open, direct and serious talk about suicidality as a core part of care, together with hope, acceptance and shared decision-making ([NVL Unipolare Depression, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)).
- NICE advises against using risk assessment tools, scales or low/medium/high risk groups to predict suicide or to decide who gets treatment ([NICE, 2022](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225)).

### What clinical guidelines say

- German S3 guideline (NVL Unipolare Depression, recommendation 12-10): at acute suicide risk, a crisis plan ("Notfallplan") **shall** be created together with the patient. This is a strong recommendation, although the evidence is rated low to very low, partly because the studies also included people without depression. Suggested parts: personal warning signs, coping strategies, not staying alone, contacts of family or friends, contacts of psychosocial services and emergency numbers, and an agreement about access to lethal means. The guideline deliberately makes no recommendation for "no-suicide contracts" ([NVL Unipolare Depression, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)).
- NICE NG225 says clinicians should **consider** developing a safety plan in partnership with people who have self-harmed. The plan should be held by the person, shared with family, carers and professionals as the person decides, and be accessible at times of crisis. The committee based this mostly on its own experience and qualitative evidence, noting that people may be too distressed to remember their plan in a crisis ([NICE, 2022](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225)).
- The German guideline also notes that outpatient crisis services are not available everywhere in Germany. GP and specialist practices and the on-call medical service are first points of contact, but are often not reachable at short notice. Clinic emergency departments or inpatient admission are often the fallback ([NVL Unipolare Depression, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)).

## Implications for Unpause

1. **Use the Stanley-Brown structure as the template.** Build the plan around the six steps in their usual order: warning signs, own coping strategies, people and places for distraction, people to ask for help, professional and emergency contacts, and making the environment safer. Both guidelines and the main evidence base use this structure ([Stanley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.1776); [NICE, 2022](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225); [NVL, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)). Do not invent a new format.
2. **Make it with the therapist, but let the user own it.** Both guidelines stress joint development. The app review found that plans built alone, without guidance, are a common gap ([Gryglewicz et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/52763)). Offer a "build it together in a session" mode through the therapist access feature. If a user starts alone, show short guidance for each step and a clear prompt to review it with their therapist. The plan belongs to the user, and they decide who sees it ([NICE, 2022](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225)). This fits the "Privacy first" principle.
3. **Always reachable, even when things are bad.** NICE says the plan must be accessible at times of crisis, and no reviewed app kept crisis contacts visible at all times ([Larsen et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0152285)). Put a safety plan button on every screen. Store the plan on the device so it works offline. Make phone numbers tap-to-call. Keep the crisis view short and readable for someone who is very distressed. If the app uses a PIN, think carefully about whether the crisis contacts should stay reachable without it.
4. **Keep it out of the gamification and the media limits.** Opening the plan should never earn or cost points, never be blocked by a media budget or digest time, and never depend on the energy check-in. This is a design judgement, not a tested finding. It follows from the guideline point that the plan must always be accessible.
5. **Verify crisis numbers and keep them current.** About one in ten reviewed apps showed wrong or dead crisis numbers, including very popular ones ([Martinengo et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-019-1461-z)). Do not hard-code numbers from memory. Keep a small, curated list of German (and later other) crisis services with source and last-checked date, and re-check it on a fixed schedule and before every release. Because outpatient crisis services are patchy in Germany ([NVL, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)), also prompt the user to add their own therapist, GP, the nearest psychiatric clinic, and the general emergency number.
6. **Include means safety, carefully.** Keep the "make my environment safer" step. Two apps in one review were missing it ([Gryglewicz et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/52763)), and both guidelines include it. Word it as an agreement made with the therapist or a trusted person (for example about storing medication). Never list methods or describe lethality. Larsen et al. found apps doing exactly that ([Larsen et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0152285)).
7. **Asking is fine, but every question needs a response path.** Asking about suicidal thoughts does not seem to increase risk ([Blades et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2018.07.001); [DeCou & Schumann, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/sltb.12368)). So the PHQ-9 check-in (whose item 9 asks about thoughts of death or self-harm) can stay. But any answer that signals risk must lead straight to the safety plan and crisis contacts. Agree with the therapist beforehand what they will and will not see, and when. Tell users plainly that Unpause is not watched in real time and is not a crisis service.
8. **No risk scores and no "we will detect your crisis" promises.** NICE advises against risk tools and risk categories for predicting suicide ([NICE, 2022](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225)). The German guideline finds no outcome evidence for passive phone or wearable monitoring ([NVL, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)). Do not show a risk level and do not imply that Unpause predicts or prevents crises on its own.
9. **Keep the plan alive with gentle, rare reminders.** Engagement with safety plan apps is low outside crises ([Gryglewicz et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/52763)), and in the Stanley study plans were reviewed and revised during follow-up calls ([Stanley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.1776)). Suggest a short review on a good day (for example every few weeks, or before a therapy session), and after the plan has been used. Keep this infrequent, in line with the "Less screen time" principle.
10. **Support follow-up, but do not replace it.** Follow-up after a crisis matters ([NVL, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf); [Doupnik et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.1586)), but brief contact on its own has mixed evidence ([Milner et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.114.147819)). After the plan has been opened, Unpause could offer a gentle check-in the next day and a nudge to contact the therapist or attend the next appointment. It should not try to act as the follow-up service itself.
11. **Do not expect the activation features to treat suicidality.** General depression tools did not significantly reduce suicidal ideation ([Torok et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2589-7500(19)30199-2)), and brief interventions did not improve depression symptoms ([Doupnik et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.1586)). Keep the safety plan a separate, focused feature. Do not frame points, routines or the media budget as protection against suicide.
12. **Decide the intended use early.** In Germany almost all app-based depression programmes exclude people with suicidality ([NVL, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)). The team should decide, with clinical and regulatory advisers, whether people at acute risk are an intended user group, and say clearly in the app what the safety plan can and cannot do.
13. **Protect the data.** The plan holds very sensitive information: warning signs, names, means agreements. Many reviewed apps lacked a privacy policy or access protection ([Larsen et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0152285)). Store the plan encrypted, share it only on the user's explicit choice, and allow the user to export or print it. A paper copy remains a sensible backup.
14. **Use a warm, hopeful, non-judgemental tone.** The German guideline stresses acceptance of suicidality as an expression of distress, hope, and avoiding both drama and trivialising ([NVL, 2022](https://www.leitlinien.de/themen/depression/langfassung/depression-vers3-2.pdf)). This matches the "Encouraging, never shaming" principle.

## Evidence gaps and risks

- **The core evidence is thin.** The main meta-analysis rests on six studies, most at high risk of bias, with signs of publication bias ([Nuij et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.2021.50)). The largest study was not randomised and mostly included male US veterans ([Stanley et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2018.1776)). The German guideline rates the evidence low to very low and notes that the studies are not specific to depression.
- **No proven effect on suicidal thoughts.** Safety planning seems to reduce suicidal behaviour, but not clearly suicidal ideation ([Nuij et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.2021.50)). Users should not be told the plan will make the thoughts go away.
- **The studied version was delivered by clinicians, often with follow-up calls.** It is unknown whether a plan a person builds alone in an app has the same effect. The role of the follow-up calls is also unclear.
- **App-based safety plans are unproven.** By 2024 there were no published RCTs of safety planning apps, only small uncontrolled pilots ([Gryglewicz et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/52763)). The one pilot RCT of an app-supported intervention found no significant effect ([Kennard et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2018.17101151)). Larger trials (for example an app-versus-paper trial in Denmark and a bigger follow-up of the ASAP/BRITE work) have been registered, but their results were not reviewed for this document. Newer results may change this picture.
- **People may not open an app in a crisis.** Engagement outside crises is low, and app use was not related to outcome in the one pilot RCT. A paper copy and saved phone contacts are reasonable backups.
- **Brief contact and means restriction.** Brief contact interventions have mixed results ([Milner et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.114.147819)). The strong evidence for means restriction is mostly at population level ([Zalsman et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(16)30030-X)). Whether a means-safety step inside an app changes behaviour has not been tested.
- **Asking about suicide.** The reassuring evidence comes mostly from research settings ([Blades et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2018.07.001); [DeCou & Schumann, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/sltb.12368)). It does not directly cover repeated questions in an app with no human reacting in real time.
- **Guideline strength.** NICE only says "consider" a safety plan and bases this mostly on committee experience ([NICE, 2022](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng225)). The German follow-up recommendations are consensus-based.
- **Possible harms:**
  - False reassurance, so that the app replaces contact with professionals.
  - Wrong or outdated crisis numbers ([Martinengo et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-019-1461-z)).
  - Harmful content, such as listing methods ([Larsen et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0152285)).
  - Users expecting a therapist to see a risk answer in real time when nobody is watching.
  - Leaks of very sensitive data.
  - A plan built alone that leaves out means safety or professional contacts.
  - The safety plan getting tangled up with gamification or media limits.
