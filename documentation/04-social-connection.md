# Social Connection

> Covers README section: 4. Stay connected

## Summary

Loneliness and low social support are consistently linked to depression. Longitudinal data suggest loneliness predicts later depression, though the link probably runs both ways and causation is not proven. Interventions that reduce loneliness have small effects. The best results come from changing unhelpful beliefs about other people, not just from creating chances for contact. Peer support for depression shows mixed results, with modest benefits at best. For Unpause, this supports small, optional, low-pressure connection nudges and a buddy feature built around a person the user already trusts and shared activities. It argues against open peer communities, leaderboards or comparison features, and against any design where success depends on someone else replying.

## Key findings

### Loneliness, social support and depression

- In a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies, adults who were often lonely had more than twice the odds of new-onset depression (pooled adjusted OR 2.33, 95% CI 1.62–3.34). Heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 64%). Most studies were in adults over 50 from the US or Europe. The authors note that causation is not established and may run both ways ([Mann et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-022-02261-7)).
- A systematic review of 100 studies (about 505,000 people) found that 90% reported a significant link between social support and protection from depression. In adults, support from a spouse or partner was the most consistent protective source, followed by family and then friends. About two thirds of the studies were cross-sectional, so reverse causation (depression reducing support) cannot be ruled out. Effects were weaker in cohort studies ([Gariépy et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.115.169094)).

### What reduces loneliness

- A meta-analysis of 50 loneliness interventions found only a small effect in randomized studies (d = −0.20). Uncontrolled studies reported larger effects, which likely overstate the benefit. Among randomized studies, the most successful interventions targeted maladaptive social cognition (unhelpful thoughts about other people and social situations), rather than only offering more contact. Samples were mostly older adults and mostly women ([Masi et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394)).
- A systematic review of 30 RCTs in people with mental health problems found the evidence too weak for firm recommendations. Cognitive approaches looked most promising for loneliness. Supported socialisation and mixed-strategy programmes looked most promising for increasing actual contact, and group formats seemed better than individual ones for that outcome. Trials in people with depression gave mixed results. Most trials were small and at high risk of bias ([Ma et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-019-01800-z)).

### Peer support for depression

- A meta-analysis found peer support reduced depressive symptoms more than usual care (7 RCTs, about 870 participants, SMD −0.59). It was not significantly different from group CBT (7 RCTs, 301 participants). However, the effect was much smaller in studies with blinded outcome assessment (−0.21) than in unblinded studies (−0.95). Many trials were in specific groups, such as postpartum women ([Pfeiffer et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.genhosppsych.2010.10.002)).
- A later meta-analysis found no effect of peer-delivered interventions on depression outcomes (4 trials, n = 695, SMD −0.12, 95% CI −0.56 to 0.32), and no effect on loneliness. Most of those trials were telephone peer support for postpartum women. The authors call the evidence base limited ([Fuhr et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-014-0857-5)).
- A recent meta-analysis of digital peer support for outpatients with mental health conditions (29 controlled studies, 5,825 participants) found small improvements in depression (SMD −0.28) and anxiety (SMD −0.47). Gains in social functioning and quality of life were also small. There was no effect on treatment engagement. The authors describe the evidence as modest and suggest digital peer support as an add-on to usual care ([Croke et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjment-2025-302275)).
- In an RCT, a closed and moderated online support group for community members with elevated distress showed no benefit at 3 months, but did reduce depression at 6 and 12 months compared with control. The forum used pseudonyms and did not allow discussion of suicide or self-harm. Engagement was low (logins in about half of all weeks) and dropout was high ([Griffiths et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0053244)).
- In an RCT with 300 adults with serious mental illness, unmoderated online peer support (email list or bulletin board) gave no benefit on any main outcome. In post-hoc analyses, more participation was linked to more distress ([Kaplan et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.09.037)).

### Reaching out

- Across a series of preregistered experiments, people consistently underestimated how much others appreciate being contacted with a short message or small gesture. The gap was larger when the contact was more surprising and when the two people were less close. These studies were in the general population, not in people with depression ([Liu et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402)).

### Buddy and accountability

- A systematic review for the US Community Preventive Services Task Force found strong evidence that social support interventions in community settings increase physical activity. Examples included buddy systems, activity contracts and walking groups. The review covered 9 studies. Median increases were 44% for time spent active and about 20% for frequency of activity. The studies date from 1980 to 2000 and mostly involved women. Evidence for family-based social support was insufficient ([Kahn et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0749-3797(02)00434-8); [Task Force finding](https://thecommunityguide.org/findings/physical-activity-social-support-interventions-community-settings)).
- The "supportive accountability" model proposes that human support improves adherence to digital interventions when the supporter is seen as trustworthy, benevolent and competent. It also requires that expectations are clear, focused on the process and agreed with the user. This is a theoretical model, not tested evidence, and it was written about coaches, not peers ([Mohr et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1602)).

### Risks

- Rejection sensitivity is moderately associated with depression (r = 0.33) and loneliness (r = 0.39) across 75 studies. The data are correlational ([Gao et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2017.08.007)).
- Co-rumination, meaning repeatedly discussing problems and negative feelings with a peer, shows a small-to-moderate link with internalizing problems such as depression (38 studies, mostly young people, cross-sectional only) ([Spendelow et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpp.2023)).
- On social networking sites, upward social comparison had a medium association with depression. This was stronger than the link between time spent online and depression. Only 5 studies covered upward comparison, and all findings are correlational ([Yoon et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2019.01.026)).
- Of 36 popular depression and smoking cessation apps, 33 sent data to third parties, mostly Google and Facebook services. This was often not disclosed in the privacy policy ([Huckvale et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.2542)).

## Implications for Unpause

### Connection nudges

- **Keep nudges small, concrete and optional.** Use prompts like "Send one message today", with graded options that match the energy check-in: react to a message, send a short text, make a call, meet up. The two-minute version can be "open the chat with one person". Loneliness interventions have small effects ([Masi et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394)). A nudge is a light behavioral activation step, not a treatment for loneliness.
- **Count the attempt, not the reply.** The task is complete when the user reaches out, whatever happens next. Never award points or show success based on a response. People with depression often expect rejection ([Gao et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2017.08.007)), and a design that depends on replies can confirm that fear.
- **Address the belief that "they don't want to hear from me".** The most effective loneliness interventions target unhelpful social beliefs ([Masi et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394); [Ma et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-019-01800-z)). A short, honest line can help, for example: "Research shows people usually appreciate hearing from someone more than the sender expects." This is based on [Liu et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402). Do not overstate it, because that research was not done in people with depression.
- **Include weak ties and old contacts.** The appreciation gap was larger for less close people ([Liu et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402)). Suggestions can include an old friend, a colleague or a neighbour, not only close family.
- **Offer optional message starters.** Short templates ("Saw this and thought of you") lower the effort of starting. Keep them editable so they don't feel scripted.
- **Use the existing mood rating.** Ask for the before and after mood rating on connection tasks too, so users can see for themselves whether contact helps. It also gives Unpause data on whether nudges are worth keeping.

### Buddy

- **Default to a person the user already knows and trusts.** Partner, family and friends are the most consistent sources of protective support ([Gariépy et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.115.169094)). Supportive accountability depends on a supporter the user sees as trustworthy and benevolent ([Mohr et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1602)).
- **Focus the buddy on doing things together.** Buddy systems and walking groups have the clearest evidence, for physical activity ([Kahn et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0749-3797(02)00434-8)). Good buddy tasks include a shared walk, a body-doubling cleanup session or a weekly check-in about plans.
- **Set expectations together and make them explicit.** Agree up front what the buddy sees and what a check-in looks like (for example, "one message on Sundays"). This follows the supportive accountability model ([Mohr et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1602)), which is still untested for peers.
- **Steer toward action, not venting.** Buddy prompts should be about plans and activities ("Want to walk at 5?"), not about rehashing problems. This lowers the risk of co-rumination ([Spendelow et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpp.2023)).
- **No leaderboards or point comparisons.** Do not show a buddy's points, streaks or progress side by side with the user's. Upward social comparison is linked to depression ([Yoon et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2019.01.026)). Share only what the user chooses, for example "Done today", not a score.

### Peer communities (not in the README, but a likely request)

- **Don't build an open peer community in early versions.** Peer support for depression has mixed and modest effects ([Pfeiffer et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.genhosppsych.2010.10.002); [Fuhr et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-014-0857-5); [Croke et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjment-2025-302275)). Unmoderated forums showed no benefit ([Kaplan et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.09.037)). If peer groups come later, they need active moderation, pseudonyms, clear rules on suicide and self-harm content, and a direct route to the safety plan. The only positive RCT here used these safeguards ([Griffiths et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0053244)).

### Privacy

- **Keep social data local and minimal.** Do not upload the user's contact list. Let users pick a contact on the device instead. Keep third-party analytics and advertising SDKs out of social features. Depression apps often leak data to third parties without disclosing it ([Huckvale et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.2542)). The buddy should only see items the user has chosen to share, and the user can revoke access at any time, as with therapist access.

## Evidence gaps and risks

- **Causation is unclear.** The links between loneliness, support and depression come from observational studies and probably run both ways ([Mann et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-022-02261-7); [Gariépy et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.115.169094)). Increasing contact will not necessarily reduce depression.
- **No direct evidence for in-app connection nudges.** None of the sources tested app prompts like "Message one person today" in people with depression. The reaching-out findings come from general-population experiments in relationships that were mostly positive ([Liu et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402)). A depressed user's message may get no reply or a cold one.
- **Loneliness interventions are modest.** Effects in randomized trials are small. Trials in people with mental health problems are small, at high risk of bias, and give mixed results for depression ([Masi et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394); [Ma et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-019-01800-z)).
- **Peer support evidence is mixed and often biased.** The larger effects shrink in blinded studies ([Pfeiffer et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.genhosppsych.2010.10.002)), one review found no effect on depression ([Fuhr et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-014-0857-5)), and digital peer support effects are small ([Croke et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjment-2025-302275)). Engagement with online groups is often low ([Griffiths et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0053244)).
- **Buddy evidence is indirect.** The strongest buddy evidence is old, mostly from women, focused on physical activity and not on depression ([Kahn et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0749-3797(02)00434-8)). The accountability model is theory ([Mohr et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1602)). No source here tested app-based buddy pairing for depression.
- **Possible harms:**
  - **Rejection:** unanswered messages may feel like proof of being unwanted, especially for people high in rejection sensitivity ([Gao et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2017.08.007)).
  - **Pressure and guilt:** repeated nudges can feel like nagging and clash with the "encouraging, never shaming" and "less screen time" principles. Limit frequency and make nudges easy to pause.
  - **Co-rumination:** two struggling people may pull each other down by dwelling on problems ([Spendelow et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpp.2023)).
  - **Comparison:** seeing a buddy's better progress may lower mood ([Yoon et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2019.01.026)).
  - **Negative peer exposure:** in unmoderated groups, more participation was linked to more distress ([Kaplan et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.09.037)).
  - **Privacy:** social features handle very sensitive data about the user and about third parties ([Huckvale et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.2542)).
  - **Crisis disclosure and buddy burden:** a buddy may receive a crisis disclosure they are not equipped to handle, or feel burdened. None of the sources studied this. The buddy flow should point to the safety plan and crisis lines, and make clear the buddy is not a therapist.

## Sources

1. Mann F et al. (2022). *Loneliness and the onset of new mental health problems in the general population*. Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-022-02261-7) — systematic review of 32 longitudinal studies, with a meta-analysis of 8 cohorts. Shows loneliness predicts later depression, the core rationale for this feature.
2. Gariépy G et al. (2016). *Social support and protection from depression: systematic review of current findings in Western countries*. The British Journal of Psychiatry. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.115.169094) — systematic review of 100 studies. Shows which sources of support are most protective, which informs who the buddy should be.
3. Masi CM et al. (2011). *A meta-analysis of interventions to reduce loneliness*. Personality and Social Psychology Review. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394) — meta-analysis of 50 studies (20 randomized). Shows effects are small and that targeting social beliefs works best, which shapes nudge wording.
4. Ma R et al. (2020). *The effectiveness of interventions for reducing subjective and objective social isolation among people with mental health problems: a systematic review*. Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-019-01800-z) — systematic review of 30 RCTs. The most relevant population for Unpause, and it shows how weak the evidence still is.
5. Pfeiffer PN et al. (2011). *Efficacy of peer support interventions for depression: a meta-analysis*. General Hospital Psychiatry. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.genhosppsych.2010.10.002) — meta-analysis of RCTs (7 vs usual care, 7 vs group CBT). Main positive evidence for peer support in depression, with clear signs of bias.
6. Fuhr DC et al. (2014). *Effectiveness of peer-delivered interventions for severe mental illness and depression on clinical and psychosocial outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis*. Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00127-014-0857-5) — meta-analysis of 14 randomized trials (4 in depression). Counterweight showing no effect of peer delivery on depression outcomes.
7. Croke S et al. (2026). *Digital peer support interventions for people with mental health conditions in outpatient settings: a systematic review and meta-analysis*. BMJ Mental Health. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjment-2025-302275) — meta-analysis of 29 controlled studies (5,825 participants). Most current estimate for digital peer support, closest to an app context.
8. Griffiths KM et al. (2012). *The effectiveness of an online support group for members of the community with depression: a randomised controlled trial*. PLoS ONE. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0053244) — RCT with 311 participants and four arms. Shows the safeguards a moderated online group used, plus low engagement.
9. Kaplan K et al. (2011). *Internet peer support for individuals with psychiatric disabilities: a randomized controlled trial*. Social Science & Medicine. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2010.09.037) — RCT with 300 participants. Shows unmoderated online peer support gave no benefit, a warning for open community features.
10. Liu PJ et al. (2023). *The surprise of reaching out: appreciated more than we think*. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402) — series of preregistered experiments (general population). Supports the "message one person" nudge and its supporting copy.
11. Kahn EB et al. (2002). *The effectiveness of interventions to increase physical activity: a systematic review*. American Journal of Preventive Medicine. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0749-3797(02)00434-8) — systematic review for the US Community Preventive Services Task Force. The Task Force finding on social support (9 studies, strong evidence) is [here](https://thecommunityguide.org/findings/physical-activity-social-support-interventions-community-settings). Best available evidence that buddy systems help behavior change.
12. Mohr DC et al. (2011). *Supportive accountability: a model for providing human support to enhance adherence to eHealth interventions*. Journal of Medical Internet Research. [Link](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1602) — theoretical model (viewpoint). Gives a framework for designing the accountability side of the buddy feature.
13. Gao S et al. (2017). *Associations between rejection sensitivity and mental health outcomes: a meta-analytic review*. Clinical Psychology Review. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2017.08.007) — meta-analysis of 75 studies. Explains why success must not depend on a reply.
14. Spendelow JS et al. (2017). *The relationship between co-rumination and internalizing problems: a systematic review and meta-analysis*. Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpp.2023) — meta-analysis of 38 cross-sectional studies. Supports keeping buddy prompts focused on action rather than on problems.
15. Yoon S et al. (2019). *Is social network site usage related to depression? A meta-analysis of Facebook–depression relations*. Journal of Affective Disorders. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2019.01.026) — four meta-analyses of correlational studies. Supports avoiding leaderboards and side-by-side progress views.
16. Huckvale K et al. (2019). *Assessment of the data sharing and privacy practices of smartphone apps for depression and smoking cessation*. JAMA Network Open. [Link](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2019.2542) — cross-sectional analysis of 36 apps. Shows why social features need strict privacy design.
